## Lede

Everything on the screen tonight is a file one of you wrote, and two days ago none of it existed.

## Thesis

This hour is the room reading its own record back to itself, in a deliberate order. First the files: three of them for the recap, then one per bridge as its writer talks, then three in full in the showcase. Then your closing paragraphs, verbatim and before anybody's theory, because they are the finding the workshop was built to produce. Only after that does the hypothesis from yesterday's close get tested — and the rule for testing it is read out before the evidence table appears, so nobody has to wonder whether the result was arranged. Where your logs and the hypothesis disagree, the room's definition is what goes into `SYNTHESIS.md`. The last thing we do is count what each of you was blind to on Monday morning, which is a number you produce rather than one we assign.

## The argument

1. Everything on screen tonight is a file one of you wrote, so your own work can do the recapping (slide 1).
2. The three-day spine is legible in three of those files, which makes the recap a reading rather than a lecture (slide 2).
3. Each bridge log then carries something only its writer can report — the target, the one thing, what a person had to supply (slide 3).
4. Three are worth seeing in full, including one that did not work, because a failed bridge is the most specific list anyone has of what a kit still lacks (slide 4).
5. Read together, your closing paragraphs are the room's own account of what a bridge turned out to be, and they come before any theory of ours (slide 5).
6. Only then is yesterday's hypothesis worth testing, with the rule read out before the evidence and your definition winning wherever the logs disagree (slide 6).
7. The same rule applies to the frame: what your practice was blind to on Monday is something you count, not something we assign (slide 7).
8. So take the repo, the open invitation and the venue, and keep going after tonight (slide 8).
