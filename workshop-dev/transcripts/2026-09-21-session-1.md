# 2026-09-21 — Session 1: kits, protocols, and the inventory exercise

The opening session of the AI Kitcraft workshop, run by the Protocol Institute's
Protocols for Business special interest group as part of Protocol Symposium 2026.
Sixteen participants across roughly eight time zones, in a Discord voice channel.

Framed at the top as experimental on two counts. First, facilitation runs **through
participants' own AI assistants** — the exercises are meant to be completed by your
assistant using your memory and your context, not by you directly. Second, the goal
is not to build a tool by the end. It is to come away understanding the technology
being used and the direction it is moving.

## Protocols as engineered arguments

The theory opened on a definition credited to Venkat from roughly a year earlier: a
protocol is an **engineered argument**.

In a conventional argument you want someone to do something, so you persuade them,
leaning on personal memory, social status or trust to get them to buy in. A protocol
externalises the argument instead — embodies it in something that exists in the
world, is durable, and diffuses as more people use it. The worked example is a
traffic light. Without one, turning left means reading other drivers' affordances and
guessing. The light externalises the argument and spreads by use.

The bridge to AI: most people's practice is a private mix of things they invented
themselves and things picked up from watching others online. Externalising that makes
it communicable, and some of what gets externalised may be worth protocolizing
properly.

Over two days the sequence kits → factories → bridges was presented as the components
of an engineered argument in the AI case: make your own use legible to yourself
first, then make it legible to others, then build components other people can talk to
without talking to you.

## The car kit analogy, and its limit

When cars were first sold they were treated as a **general-purpose portable power
source**. Farmers built kits to take that power and apply it elsewhere — plough
attachments, pulley rigs for churning butter, washing machines driven off the engine.
Some early washing machine designs inherited that lineage, down to pedal-like
controls.

Specific-purpose products eventually displaced the kits. Ford's first tractor arrived
around 1917–18, but people kept building kits well into the 1940s, and it was only in
the 1950s that kit-building fell away in favour of purpose-built tractors and trucks.

The analogy was then deliberately undercut. Cars arrived into a low-protocolization
world — no Federal Highway Act until 1955–56, no interstate system, comparatively
little traffic law. AI arrives into an environment already dense with inherited
protocols from the internet and from everyday working practice. A closer metaphor
might be a new car technology landing in the 1970s or 80s, which is the world of
Ballard's *Crash* and *Concrete Island* — the latter a modern retelling of Robinson
Crusoe in which the protagonist is stranded on a concrete median in the middle of a
motorway and cannot work out how to leave.

## The maturity model: governed and cultivated

Two axes. **Governed** is top-down: what organisations sanction, mandate and budget.
**Cultivated** is bottom-up: how individuals and small groups actually adapt the
technology.

The claim: organisations are roughly at the second governed level — access is
sanctioned, tools are approved, token budgets exist, but there is no protocolization
of *use*. Individuals are at the **kit level**: personal workflows that are
solipsistic and one-to-one, and correspondingly hard to translate to a team.

A kit was defined as a local protocol for adapting AI inside a system that is already
heavily prototyped. Its components are recognisable — skill and agent markdown files,
worked examples handed to the model to show the shape of output wanted, and the
practices assembled around them.

## Three affordances

- **Automation** — repetitive work. The familiar one, and the one most participants
  arrived already using.
- **Spookification** — bespoke, one-to-one tools built fast and thrown away. The
  example offered was a retrieval agent built for research on a single book, used
  only for that, with no expectation of reuse.
- **Pensification** — sharing raw outputs and interfaces inside small trusted groups,
  including ways for other people to talk to things you have built.

## Pre-commodification

Everyone queries the same substrate. Two people researching the same question can
converge on the same answer without ever speaking, so **common knowledge forms
invisibly**. The model behaves like an index fund: you receive an answer with no sense
of how many others arrived at the same place. Disputes over who "really" first
resolved a given mathematical conjecture were offered as a live symptom.

A second consequence: with AI you end up sharing not just finished outputs but the
progression that produced them. The analogy offered was sharing every draft and every
variation of every email you ever wrote, rather than the sent version. Those
intermediate products turn out to be valuable in their own right — you can fork
someone's half-formed idea and build a different product from it, which is closer to
sharing memory than to sharing work.

A third: because bespoke tools are so cheap to build, **reinventing a kit is often
cheaper than adopting and customising someone else's**. That inverts the
collaboration habits of the last decade. The goal becomes making it easy for others
to reinvent, and broadcasting what you have found useful, rather than distributing a
finished artefact.

## The hard question

New interfaces are appearing — single-project websites, single-project chatbots,
custom connectors for applications with a lifespan of three or seven days. The
underlying movement was framed as **journal to field notebook**: right now everyone's
assistant is a private journal, customised to them and used alone.

Which sets up the question the rest of the workshop exists to attack: if everyone is
hyper-customised and isolated, how do you make your own work observable and
engageable from the outside? Stigmergy and public traces were flagged as the
direction of travel, to be taken up in the following sessions.

## From the room

**Organisational blockers.** The two looming obstacles to adoption were identified as
**liability rules** and **market power**. On liability: the exposure an organisation
carries for actions now mediated by AI tooling. On market power: privacy and
confidentiality, not inadvertently handing your moat to a provider, and
platform-level lock-in where a toolchain presumes a particular host. An observation
that stuck — anyone in a leadership role who is not using these tools daily is
already behind the curve, because they are making many other decisions and this one
is being made without direct experience.

**Standardisation or fragmentation.** A participant with a background lecturing in
software engineering, now working in a team of about twenty developers alongside
academics, raised the open question: will shared tools drag everyone into a
standardised way of working, or will the tools fragment into many bespoke practices?
The observation underneath it was that apprentices coming from large, very formal
engineering organisations all claimed standardised process and all did it
differently. They explicitly declined to bet either way.

The same participant offered what became the session's most-used metaphor: a horse
trek in Belize where it took an hour to realise the riders had no real control over
the horses at all. The animals had walked the route so many times they were
determined to stick to it. You could steer a little and move around each other, but
you could not go somewhere else. The only real controls were stop and start. It was
picked up immediately as a description of working with a pre-commodified model.

**Shared hosted computation.** A participant described regularly using a friend's
astrology skill rather than building their own, because it connects to a server the
friend hosts that does the real ephemeris calculation. The boundary this identifies:
as soon as something requires hosted computation, borrowing becomes more valuable
than rolling your own — which is precisely where the reinvent-the-wheel logic stops
holding.

**Forcing the assistant to log.** Another participant described a logging system that
requires Claude to record every decision and research step into structured daily
files, rather than letting the model decide what is worth saving. Shared as a plugin
when onboarding others.

**Language as interface.** LLMs turn applications into language-first interfaces,
which makes vocabulary load-bearing. One participant described an
aesthetic-description-driven approach: feed in references plus a described aesthetic
assembled from several distinct visual traditions, and the model gets what you mean —
provided you know the references well enough to name them. The trade-off drawn out in
discussion: the further a domain is from your own expertise, the more valuable
someone else's kit becomes, because you lack the jargon to specify what you want.

**Adoption patterns.** The hydraulic backhoe story from *The Innovator's Dilemma* was
raised as a precedent — a technology that could not win the large contracts it first
targeted, found a foothold in small jobs that the incumbent steam and coal machines
could not serve economically, and eventually became the dominant architecture.

## The exercise

Ask your assistant, using your own history and memory, to identify around five AI
tools or kits you use regularly or have built yourself, summarise them, and push the
summary to the shared repository.

The rationale given: AI abstracts things into the background, and you lose awareness
of the automated kits and workflows you depend on. Surfacing them makes them
discussable, which is a precondition for sharing them. The second-order goal is a
shared inventory — overlaps between participants, or several people who have
independently rebuilt the same tool.

The live demonstration failed on first attempt, for a reason specific to the
demonstrator's machine, and was noted at the time as an illustration of the only
reliable rule about live demos. It worked on the retry, and the repository
instructions were updated mid-session to smooth the setup problems participants were
hitting.

## Friction worth recording

Getting a group onto the same stack is harder than it looks and was underestimated.
Discord is a gate, because many people are not on it. GitHub is a second gate:
accounts, collaborator invitations, accepting them, confirming push access. Several
participants were working through these during the theory.

A related prediction from the room: the harnesses will absorb version control.
Within about a year, Claude and Codex may each have their own git engine — possibly
running a partnership underneath — so that you sign in and get a project identifier,
and the repository layer is abstracted away entirely.

## Institute context, for the record

The Protocol Institute grew out of Summer of Protocols, begun in 2022 with Ethereum
Foundation funding to study protocols as a first-class subject, and launched in its
current form in May 2026. Five special interest groups meet fortnightly: protocols
for business, memory research, personhood, formal protocol theory, and protocol
fiction. The institute maintains a retrieval bot trained on its own material, which
by this session already included the workshop material. Written output appears on the
institute's publication and on a facilitator's own newsletter.

## Close

Next sessions to be far more building-focused: different interfaces, stigmergy and
leaving public traces, and the distinction between a **kit** — private, journal-like —
and a **factory**, which is legible and runnable without the context of whoever built
it. Optional office hours offered an hour later in the same room for setup
troubleshooting.
