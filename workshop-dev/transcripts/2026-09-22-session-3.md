# 2026-09-22 — Session 3: factories, shared language, and demos

Day two. Demos of participant-built software, then the session's real subject: the
vocabulary problem that appears as soon as anyone tries to hand their work to
somebody else.

## What a factory is, argued through a demo

The session opened on a distributed robot-hiring project. Someone submits a task or
request for proposal in plain English; it is converted into machine-readable tasks;
robots evaluate those against their own capabilities and decide whether to bid; the
winner is paid, executes, and returns the gathered data. All of it on blockchain
rails, so anyone can build their own marketplace on top. It works end to end on
mainnet — registration, bidding, winning, payment, execution. A teleoperation
interface for the robots was demonstrated live. Alongside it, an LLM pipeline
converting English PDFs into machine-readable robot tasks, which is the harder and
less finished half.

A participant asked the useful question directly: why call this a factory rather than
just software for controlling robots?

The answer sharpened the definition. A **factory is not being built at commercial
scale for other people.** It is a heavily protocolized process built for yourself, or
for your friends, so that you can run your own explorations reliably. The moment you
start describing it as a product or a platform you get pulled into a different design
paradigm, with different obligations, and the thing you were actually making is lost.

The complementary framing: a factory is an **extension of a single person** —
embodied skills turned into software that can run without them, but still recognisably
theirs, with the automation, densification and bespoke-creation affordances layered
on. A single-player phenomenon that does not need you present.

And the economic argument underneath: these are objects that, under the previous
paradigm, **should not exist**. They were not cost-effective in time, money or skill,
so they simply were not built. They are more than toys — hardened enough that a
person or a small community can build a life on top of them — but not at scale, and
not quite open-source software either.

A participant connected this to the 1980s home-electronics movement: component shops
on every high street, hobbyists building their own devices, all of it gone once
products matured. The facilitators' stated bet is the same curve — a couple of
decades in which bespoke micro-factory building is cost-effective and widespread,
after which a new class of products absorbs it and the next generation tinkers less
because it no longer needs to.

One prediction attached to that: what is easiest to turn into a factory is whatever
you already have deep context in — years of domain experience, a long-running
project. And model companies tend to productise exactly the high-context domains
where many people have already built the same personal tool.

## The Tower of Babel problem

The session's recurring theme, raised persistently by one participant and picked up
by everyone.

Every personal tool and harness develops **its own domain language**, invented by
whoever built it. The result is that neither people nor agents can easily understand
or integrate each other's projects. The proposal on the table was that software may
need a *parent* domain language — a shared vocabulary within which individual
projects can be described — and, more speculatively, something like a design or
art-history education for software, so practitioners share an aesthetic vocabulary
the way architects do. Software was argued to be uniquely prone to this because it
permits arbitrary naming of anything.

This was not abstract. A facilitator described finding it genuinely hard to gain
context on another participant's kit, and having to ask Claude to translate it before
it made sense.

The condition was named as an **uncanny valley**: a tool customised for you fails on
both counts at once — not customised enough to be worth handing over, not general
enough to be a product. The pattern that follows was described from experience: a
team member builds an excellent kit, nobody can adopt it because the language is
idiosyncratic and it is over-fitted to its author, nobody adopts it, and nobody tells
them. The expectation offered was roughly a decade of this before convergence.

## How emerging fields solve it

Crypto was offered as the precedent, having solved the same problem twice over: a
standardised language for protocol upgrades, and the conference circuit acting as a
consensus mechanism for terminology. The conference circuit was argued to be badly
undervalued in that account.

The expectation is convergence by the same route — new standards bodies, in verticals
that may not exist yet. Until then, in-person and peer-to-peer co-building of the
kind this workshop was attempting becomes more important, not less, because it is the
only thing doing the vocabulary work.

This was framed as a **Gramsci gap** — an interval between paradigms, a time of
monsters — after which the new platform's ontology dominates and the history gets
rewritten as though the products had always been there. The analogy drawn: the
forgotten chaos of competing internet protocols in the 1990s, now remembered as
though the winners were inevitable.

A counter-example was offered from a water-data project presented earlier in the
symposium. Different water bodies measured and reported on incompatible standards,
which made water rates effectively incomprehensible. The solution was not another
standard on top. It was **translation between the standards that already existed** —
which may be the more realistic model for this problem than waiting for convergence.

## Writing for an audience improves the work

The practical technique of the session, and the one with the widest application.

The prompt pattern: tell your assistant to clean this up, strip the personal
information, add a README and an orientation, and prepare it so a friend can use it —
then push it to a shared repository.

The reason it works is not tidiness. Forcing the assistant to produce something that
runs **without your local history, memory, context and toolchain** surfaces bugs and
generalises the tool. The assistant will tell you what it cannot do because it
depends on your working directory, and then generalise it. The lesson was learned the
hard way, by actually sending something to a friend and discovering it was broken for
anyone but its author.

The observation that generated the most agreement: the assistant enters a **different
mode of creation** when told the output is for someone else, and produces materially
better work than when it is working with you alone.

A participant drew the analogy to writing itself, and to why academic conferences
persist: externalising thought for an audience imposes an order that ordinary
cognition does not. Even a journal is written for a future reader. The same
participant described deliberately telling one model that its draft would be reviewed
by a competing model, and feeling the outputs improved — while flagging honestly that
this might be anthropomorphising.

Two further analogies from the room: hosting a weekly dinner, which makes you cook
better, clean the house and assemble a clearer account of your week; and the shift in
register that happens when a draft sits next to a publish button.

Both facilitators noted the difficulty of the reverse problem — explaining concepts
to newcomers that they have internalised over eighteen months of talking mainly to
each other.

## Other projects shown or described

- A **file-organisation kit** built by pointing Claude Code at years of accumulated
  downloads and near-duplicate drafts. Presented as deliberately banal, and a good
  illustration of the uncanny valley: nominally a one-off cleanup, but because the
  scaffolding is cheap to build you go further and ask whether it can run again on
  future files, and across other archives.
- **Sprungbrett**, an MVP to help immigrants in Germany with job search, prototyped
  out of the previous day's breakout. The premise: many German job listings state
  German-language requirements that the role does not actually require, so there is a
  gap between what is advertised and what is reachable. The demo matches roles against
  qualifications rather than language alone, asks a short set of follow-up questions —
  shift work, relocation, driving licence, contract type — and returns suggestions
  such as which language level to target next or which credential to get recognised,
  in the user's own language. Motivated by the experience of employment offices being
  effectively inaccessible to people who do not speak German.
- **The Prime Radiant**, from the psychohistory special interest group: history read
  through technological evolution, organised around "world machines" — a modernity
  machine that produced standard practices, a current divergent-machine phase of
  coexisting plural worlds — plus an almanac forecasting the technological paradigms
  that might emerge within a lifetime, and an oracle for asking counterfactuals. Cited
  as work that could only be attempted now that history can be synthesised at scale.

The point drawn from all of them: the cost of building software has collapsed, so the
scarce resource is no longer capability. It is **imagination about what to build**.
