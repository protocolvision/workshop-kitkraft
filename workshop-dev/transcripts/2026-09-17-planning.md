# 2026-09-17 — Facilitator planning call

Rafa and Sachin. A working call, four days before the workshop, walking the full
four-session arc end to end and testing whether it held together.

## The arc, as it stood

**Session 1 — kits.** Opens on the observation that different interfaces are emerging
between individuals, communities and machinery, and that none of it has settled. The
one thing that is clear is that collaboration requires signalling, and that there are
several kinds of signal. This introduces **stigmergy** as the dominant mode of
collaboration in AI: you are not handing work off, you are broadcasting the work
itself — traces of it, your logs, the production system, the infrastructure you use.

The step back from there: what people are actually doing is moving a **private
practice into a public practice**, and collaboration is emerging from that rather
than from deliberate workflow design in the way agile or waterfall were designed.
The claim on the table was that this is *the* collaboration paradigm, not one of
several.

Two illustrations were proposed. A personal journal set against a field notebook —
the point being that ethnographers and birdwatchers collaborate not only by planning
expeditions together but by publishing field notes with no purpose beyond publishing
them. And Humboldt's cross-section of a mountain, as an example of a diagram that
does this work.

The exercise: ask your own assistant to surface the tools you use, including ones you
are not aware of using, then read other people's inventories looking for tools near
or overlapping your own. Overlapping traces are what make the signal strong enough to
act on. The session closes into joint troubleshooting.

**Session 2 — running a kit without its author.** The central question is whether a
kit can run without the author's oversight: what it takes to move from a bespoke
thing on your own machine to something where *you* are the interchangeable part.
Exercise in two halves — first articulate what the tool still needs you for, then
work out what hard protocols or standards would translate that away. Automating
yourself out of your own kit.

Theory follows on the reorganisation of factories after electrification, with a
reframing that came up in the call: the usual telling focuses on how the production
line was rearranged, but the more interesting lens is how *humans* were transformed
by electricity into a different kind of worker, which is what then allowed the
factory to reorganise itself. Both agreed this is a deeper point than the workshop
needs, and that the simpler version would be taught.

**Factories.** A factory makes humans interchangeable in the production process. It
is not defined by its form — not a single file, not a particular scale; it can be an
app or a website. Two in-house examples were cited as things reaching toward the
definition without necessarily meeting it. The exercise is to have your agent help
industrialise a specific kit.

**Session 3 — bridges.** Once factories run on their own they become networked. The
exercise combines two kits, or a factory and a kit, to produce a single output —
composing a network rather than a chain. It is the inverse of the session-2 exercise:
that one removes you from the kit, this one connects kits to each other.

**Session 4 — show and tell.** Participants present a kit, a factory or a bridge.

## What the design was still working out

Several open questions remained about how the theory section was sequenced — in
particular where the material on customisation and pre-commodification should sit
relative to the two-by-two and the exercise, and whether a closing reflection round
asking what participants had noticed would land. That reflection idea was considered
interesting but optional.

On the factory exercise, the suggestion was made that it needed tighter constraints
to fit a 60–90 minute slot. The proposed simplification: drop the build component
entirely, so the exercise is to identify a kit and draw a road map toward a factory
rather than to construct one. Using a job-search kit as the worked example, two
different reconceptualisations were offered — one where the factory produces
retrieval agents for job searching, and one where the industry or the person using
the agent becomes the swappable part.

## Whether four sessions is enough

The most common piece of external feedback had been scepticism that this fits into
four sessions. The response offered: we are miscalibrating what a workshop is when
participants' assistants are executing the exercises. There is no hard limit; the
constraint is how much compute people are willing to spend.

## Persistence as the thing that makes this different

Both facilitators kept returning to the same point. A typical workshop ends, notes
get shelved, and the context is almost never used again. This one persists in the
participants' own codebases. Everyone working in the repo ends up holding a slice of
everyone else's memory. The institute retains an output. And the next run forks the
current workshop stack into a new repo and improves on the feedback rather than
starting over.

The append-only logs are part of that. Copying the template into a participant's
folder creates a log; agents working in the repo add their own lines; feedback a
participant gives their harness gets written into the same file and folded back into
the work. Each harness has instructions to keep its log current.
