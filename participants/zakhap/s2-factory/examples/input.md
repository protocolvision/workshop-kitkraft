# Corpus: the Ruskin–Whistler quarrel (1877–1885)

Two sources, public domain. Ruskin's notice of Whistler's *Nocturne in Black and Gold*
provoked a libel suit; Whistler won a farthing in damages and no costs, and was bankrupted.
They are arguing about the same three words from opposite ends, which is what makes this
a usable example corpus rather than a glossary.

---

source: John Ruskin, *Fors Clavigera*, Letter 79, 2 July 1877

> For Mr. Whistler's own sake, no less than for the protection of the purchaser, Sir Coutts
> Lindsay ought not to have admitted works into the gallery in which the ill-educated conceit
> of the artist so nearly approached the aspect of wilful imposture. I have seen, and heard,
> much of Cockney impudence before now; but never expected to hear a coxcomb ask two hundred
> guineas for flinging a pot of paint in the public's face.

---

source: John Ruskin, *Modern Painters*, vol. I, 1843

> They should go to Nature in all singleness of heart, and walk with her laboriously and
> trustingly, having no other thoughts but how best to penetrate her meaning; rejecting
> nothing, selecting nothing, and scorning nothing.

---

source: James McNeill Whistler, testimony in *Whistler v. Ruskin*, November 1878

> ATTORNEY-GENERAL: The labour of two days is that for which you ask two hundred guineas?
>
> WHISTLER: No, I ask it for the knowledge of a lifetime.

---

source: James McNeill Whistler, "The Red Rag", *The World*, 1878; repr. *The Gentle Art of Making Enemies*

> Art should be independent of all clap-trap — should stand alone, and appeal to the artistic
> sense of eye or ear, without confounding this with emotions entirely foreign to it, as
> devotion, pity, love, patriotism, and the like.

---

source: James McNeill Whistler, the "Ten O'Clock" lecture, 1885

> Nature contains the elements, in colour and form, of all pictures, as the keyboard contains
> the notes of all music. But the artist is born to pick, and choose, and group with science,
> these elements, that the result may be beautiful.

> Nature is very rarely right, to such an extent even, that it might almost be said that
> Nature is usually wrong.
